Vocabulary

What good communicators do

They really listen and seldom interrupt. If they don't understand, they wait for a chance to clarify. They never confuse the listener, and they explain jargon with an easy example. They may digress (leave the main topic for a moment) but never ramble (talk without direction). They engage with colleagues, employees, customers and suppliers.

Trap: “in the right tense and form”

  • stop / avoid / blamed for + -ing → stop beating about the bush
  • can't / managed to / be able to + base form → cannot pass the buck, managed to be on the same wavelength
  • a past story → couldn't make head or tail of it, were talking at cross purposes, got the wrong end of the stick
  • must have + past participle → must have got our wires crossed
  • change the person → put you / them in the picture, got their wires crossed

Word families

Read the gap to pick the form

  • After a / the / 's / of, “a great deal of”, “showing” → noun: the presenter's fluency, a great deal of responsiveness
  • After be / become / feel / seem / very, or before a noun → adjective: become more assertive, feel inhibited, a laid-back company
  • Describing a verb → adverb: they express their ideas coherently
  • Adverb + past participle: formally dressed, formally behaved
  • over- means too much: over-cautious
  • Opposites: incoherent, insensitive, unresponsive, indecisive, discourteous, unrealistic

Grammar

6 min
Grammar

A · Past simple vs present perfect

DS 2023DS 2025

Past simple: finished, time known

He booked the venue two weeks ago. I sent you the agenda yesterday.

yesterday · last week · two weeks ago · in 2024 · When…? · What happened? · Last time we met…

Present perfect: linked to now

We've hit a problem. I haven't made a final choice yet.

already · yet · just · ever · never · recently · so far · up to now · to date · since… · for… · since our last meeting · over the last few months

Either tense: today, this morning, this week, in the last month. Use the present perfect if the period is still going on, the past simple if it is over. already = earlier than expected. yet = we expect it to happen.

  1. A When…? question is always past simple: When did you see him?
  2. The story after What happened? stays in the past simple: he rejected anything that looked new… he dropped out of the team.
  3. since + a moment → present perfect: I have been with Lescot since January (not “I am”). The moment itself is past simple: since I began working for them.
  4. for + a period → I haven't seen you for months.
  5. yet / already / just → present perfect: he hasn't made up his mind yet · I've just organized that.
  6. A result now, with no time given → present perfect: You have been very efficient.

Grammar

C · The passive

corrected in class this year

be (in the right tense) + past participle

is proposed · was taken · has been agreed · will be coordinated

Why: more formal · who did it doesn't matter · you don't know who · to distance yourself from the decision

Two patterns

tell, inform, instruct, persuade (+ a person): the person becomes the subject. I've been told that… · We've been instructed to…

suggest, agree, decide, propose (no person): start with It. It has been suggested that… Never I've been suggested.

Class exercise teacher's key

  1. Our new deadline is 31 January (inform) → We have been informed that our new official deadline is the 31st of January.
  2. Management think we should cut back on consultants (agree) → It has been agreed that we should cut back on our use of freelance consultants.
  3. The department will be restructured (propose) → It has been proposed that the department (should) be restructured next year.
  4. Head Office wants us to cut spending by 5% (instruct) → We have been instructed to reduce our spending by 5%.
  5. Bonuses twice a year from now on (decide) → It has been decided that bonuses will be paid twice a year from now on.
  6. We're taking part in a system trial (persuade) → We have been persuaded to take part in a new system trial.

Open question DS 2023Write 5–6 sentences and reuse course words. Ready-made answers are below.

Attitude words. Positive: enthusiastic, impressed, optimistic. Negative: critical, disappointed, sceptical, frustrated. Neutral: objective, balanced.

Ready-made open answers

What makes a project succeed?

Besides delivering on time, a project needs upfront planning, an accurate forecast and a realistic budget. The manager should prioritize tasks, keep track of spending and check progress regularly, so the team stays on track and within budget. Clear communication with the team, suppliers and clients prevents breakdowns, and conflicts must be resolved quickly. Finally, reviewing past projects stops the team from repeating the same mistakes.

What makes a good communicator?

A good communicator really listens and does not interrupt. They are articulate, coherent and succinct, so they get straight to the point instead of rambling. They avoid jargon or explain it with an easy example, pay attention to body language, and are responsive and sensitive to other people. As Wittgenstein said, everything that can be said can be said clearly.

What makes a good leader?

“The key to successful leadership today is influence, not authority” (Kenneth Blanchard). A good leader shows integrity and empathy, is decisive but collaborative, and is approachable rather than distant. They take on responsibility instead of passing the buck, and they shape and share a vision, which gives point to the work of others (Charles Handy).

Ten traps to check before you hand in

1 min
  1. “Right tense or form” means you change the verb: ran out of money, allocating funds, were talking at cross purposes.
  2. stop / avoid / blamed for + -ing: stop beating about the bush.
  3. When…? and finished times take the past simple. yet, already, just, since, for take the present perfect.
  4. Commas around a relative clause: who or which, never that.
  5. Not I've been suggested. Write It has been suggested that…
  6. Opposite pairs: cautious ↔ decisive, casual ↔ formal, idealistic ↔ realistic.
  7. Prepositions: run into · come in for · fall behind · go over · stay within · put forward.
  8. “There are 3 extra words”: cross each word out as you use it.
  9. True / False: always correct the false ones with the text's words.
  10. Spelling: succinct, eloquence, responsiveness, laid-back (with a hyphen), formally dressed. Never leave a gap empty.

Team building

Stages: Forming (anxious, depends on the leader) → Storming (conflict) → Norming (closer, conflicts settled) → Performing (doing the task, flexible roles) → Mourning or Adjourning (the team breaks up).

Roles: Plant (creative ideas) · Teamworker (diplomatic, prevents conflict) · Co-ordinator (clarifies goals, delegates) · Shaper (dynamic, pushes past obstacles) · Completer Finisher (meticulous, quality and deadlines).

Prefixes: mis- badly (mismanage) · pro- in favour of · pre- before · post- after · ex- former · bi- two · re- again · hyper- very or too much · dis-, un-, in-, im-, ir- not (dishonest, uncommunicative, indecisive, impractical, irresponsible).

Modal perfect (modal + have + past participle). Criticising the past: should have, shouldn't have, needn't have, could have. Guessing: must have, may have, might have, couldn't have.

Ethical business

Collocations: act responsibly · comply with regulations · share a strong commitment to · take an active part in · stay true to its principles · reduce the impact · donate time or money.

Ethical: ethics, responsibility, fairness, generosity, values, credibility. Unethical: bribery, deception, corruption, greed, discrimination, prejudice.

Future forms: will = decision while speaking, or prediction · going to = plan · present continuous = arrangement · present simple = timetable.

Making decisions

Personality types: extrovert or introvert · sensor or intuitive · thinker or feeler · judger or perceiver.

Phrases: weigh up all the options · consider different perspectives · trust my instincts · rely on my own judgement · have confidence in my decision.

good at a skill · good with people or things.

Countability: information, advice, equipment, travel, news, money and software are uncountable (much, little, less). Facts, proposals and journeys are countable (many, few, fewer).
